Low-Load Distributed Data Networks: Concept, Classification and Comparative Analysis

Author of the concept: Kolpakov Andrey

Version: 30 September 2026

Related: Zero Knowledge Infrastructure manifesto, comparison of networks.

Abstract

This article introduces the concept of a low-load distributed data network: a network in which the load on each node (traffic, storage, service state) is bounded by that node's own circle of participants and does not grow as the network as a whole grows. We show that the load classifications used in communications theory (utilization, erlangs, the "knee" and "cliff" points on the throughput-versus-load curve, quality-of-service classes) describe the state of a network at a given moment rather than a property of its architecture, and therefore cannot distinguish networks by this trait. We propose six traits of a low-load network and a classification LL-0…LL-3 by the asymptotic load on a node. We compare fifteen networks, from FidoNet (1984) to Nevod (2026), and name their predecessors in the literature: friend-to-friend (F2F) networks, delay-tolerant networks (DTN), distributed hash tables, local-first software and community networks.

Keywords: distributed networks, decentralization, per-node load, scalability, F2F, DTN, peer-to-peer networks, messengers, Zero Knowledge Infrastructure.

1. Introduction

Most of today's messaging services are centralized: the whole network is served by one company's servers, and the load on those servers grows with the number of users. Decentralized alternatives (federated networks, peer-to-peer overlays, anonymity networks) distribute ownership of the infrastructure but, as shown below, usually not the load: a federation server or overlay node carries traffic, history and service state produced by other people's users, and its load again grows with the size of the network or of its popular part.

The aim of this work is to single out the class of networks in which a node's load is determined only by its own circle: the users and software agents (bots, AI agents, services) for which it is the home node; to define this class, to propose a measurable classification and to compare existing networks by this trait.

2. Load in data networks: existing concepts

2.1 Utilization

In queueing theory the load of a channel or node is characterized by the utilization factor ρ = λ/μ, the ratio of the arrival rate to the service rate [1]. As ρ → 1, queue length and delay grow without bound; at ρ > 1 the system is overloaded. In practice rough levels are used: low utilization (ρ < ~0.3), moderate (0.3–0.7), high (0.7–0.9), saturation and overload; operators usually plan links with headroom up to ~70–80% at peak hour. These thresholds are engineering guidelines, not a single standard.

2.2 Teletraffic: erlangs and the busy hour

In telephony, traffic intensity is measured in erlangs (1 Erl is one channel occupied continuously) [2]; dimensioning is based on the busy hour; offered, carried and lost traffic are distinguished, and the grade of service is set as an acceptable blocking probability (Erlang B and C formulas) [1, 2].

2.3 How a network reacts to growing load: the "knee" and the "cliff"

Jain, Ramakrishnan and Chiu [3] described three operating regions of a network: before the "knee", throughput grows with load at low delay; between the "knee" and the "cliff", throughput barely grows while delay rises quickly; past the "cliff", congestion and collapse set in. At the "knee" the ratio of throughput to delay (the network's "power") is at its maximum; this distinction underlies congestion avoidance (keeping operation near the "knee") and congestion control (preventing a fall off the "cliff"). TCP congestion control is built on these ideas [4].

2.4 Traffic and quality-of-service classes

The Differentiated Services architecture [5] introduces per-hop behaviors (Expedited Forwarding EF, Assured Forwarding AF, Best Effort BE); ITU-T Recommendation Y.1541 [6] defines IP network QoS classes 0–5 by delay, jitter and loss. These are classifications of requirements for delivery, not of load.

2.5 Load regimes: from underload to collapse

Network operating regimes by load are described in standards and classic works:

RegimeWhere definedMeaning
Underload (underutilization)descriptive, not defined in standardslinks are noticeably idle; often a deliberate capacity reserve (overprovisioning)
Normal loadITU-T E.500 [36]frequent busy periods in which user expectations should be met; target grade-of-service values are set for it
High loadITU-T E.500 [36]infrequent peak periods: expectations may not be met, but performance must stay high enough to keep congestion from spreading through the network
OverloadITU-T E.412 [37]; accounted for in GOST standards on communication networks [39]traffic exceeds the network's capacity; management controls kick in: automatic congestion control, circuit reservation, rerouting
Congestion collapseRFC 896, J. Nagle, 1984 [38]every packet is sent many times and useful throughput drops almost to zero; first observed in October 1986, when the NSFnet backbone fell from 32 kbit/s to 40 bit/s

There is no established term for a "fully loaded" network; the level a network is dimensioned for corresponds to "normal load" in E.500.

All these regimes are states of a network at a given moment: the same network is underloaded in the morning, at normal load in the evening and overloaded during an outage. The axis of regimes and the axis of architectural classes introduced below are independent: a network of class LL-0 can also become overloaded, for example if a node sits on a weak link.

2.6 Scaling of per-node load

In the distributed-systems literature a node's cost is assessed asymptotically as a function of the number of participants N: broadcasting to all nodes costs O(N) per message; distributed hash tables (Chord [7], Kademlia [8]) cost O(log N) for routing and the neighbor table. There is also the claim that in peer-to-peer systems the maximum load per node does not grow with the number of nodes, because new nodes bring more resources than they consume [9], but this refers to statistical balancing of a load made up of other people's requests.

2.7 Conclusion

Classifications 2.1–2.5 answer the question "how loaded is the network now"; by them the same network is sometimes lightly and sometimes heavily loaded. The Russian phrase "lightly loaded network" (малонагруженная сеть) is used in practice exactly in this descriptive sense (a network or machine with low utilization) and is not a term for an architectural class. In cable telephony the word "loading" even has a third meaning: a "loaded line" with inductance coils (Pupin loading). Only axis 2.6 concerns a property of the architecture, and there is no standard classification of networks along it.

3. Distributed networks: an overview of approaches

Centralized services (WhatsApp, 2009; Telegram, 2013; Signal, 2014 [10]): end-to-end encryption of content can be strong, but servers, accounts and metadata are held by one organization; server load grows with the entire audience.

Federated networks: e-mail, Matrix (2014) [11], ActivityPub/Mastodon (2016; W3C Recommendation 2018) [12]. Ownership is distributed among servers, but a server's load grows with its external connections: in Matrix the history of a large room is replicated to every participating server, in ActivityPub delivery grows with the number of followers.

Peer-to-peer overlays and distributed hash tables: Freenet (2001) [13], Chord [7], Kademlia [8]. Nodes are equal, load is O(log N), but a node stores and routes data of strangers.

Friend-to-friend (F2F) networks. The term was proposed by D. Bricklin in 2000 [14]: a node connects only to the nodes of people it knows. Examples: Turtle (Popescu, Crispo, Tanenbaum, 2004) [15], OneSwarm [16], Freenet's "darknet" mode, RetroShare, GNUnet; storage on trusted nodes also belongs here [17]. A node's number of connections is bounded by its number of friends [18, 19], but messages travel along chains of friends through uninterested nodes, and routing in such networks remains a hard problem [18, 19, 20]. F2F networks are the closest predecessor of the proposed concept: they were the first to bound a node by its circle of acquaintances, but by the number of connections, not by load.

Anonymity overlay networks (onion routing): a development of D. Chaum's mix networks (1981) [40] and the onion routing of D. Goldschlag, M. Reed and P. Syverson (1996) [41]; implementations include Tor (2002–2004) [21] and Session (2020) [22]. Relay nodes run by volunteers or operators carry other people's transit in proportion to their bandwidth.

Delay-tolerant networks (DTN): Fall's architecture (2003) [23], RFC 4838 [24], Bundle Protocol v7, RFC 9171 [25]: "store and forward" across disrupted links. A historical implementation of the same principle is FidoNet (1984) [26], a modern one is NNCP (2016) [27].

Stacks for any medium and radio networks: Reticulum (protocol published in 2016) and the LXMF messenger [28]: operation from a few bits per second (LoRa, packet radio) up to the internet, encryption by default; Meshtastic (2020) [29]: text messaging over LoRa radio modules.

Community networks: Freifunk, Guifi.net [30]: networks created and run by their users themselves.

Local-first software [31] and Secure Scuttlebutt (2014) [32]: data lives with the user; in SSB a node stores the feeds of its friends and friends of friends, and synchronization happens when nodes meet.

Relays: Nostr (2020) [33]: simple relay servers, the popular ones loaded by the whole audience.

4. The concept of a low-load distributed network

4.1 Definition

A low-load distributed data network is a network in which each node serves a small, naturally bounded circle of participants and carries mostly their own traffic. Nodes are equal and run on ordinary hardware. The network transmits only what is necessary, tolerates delays and link disruptions, and has no center whose shutdown or seizure would stop it.

The concept concerns the design of a network, not its load at a given moment (sections 2.5, 2.7).

4.2 Traits

  1. Own circle: a node's load grows with its circle, the people and software agents it serves, not with the size of the whole network. The main trait.
  2. Ordinary hardware: a home computer, mini-server or router; no data center needed.
  3. Transmission economy: text and signals first, bulky data on request.
  4. Delay tolerance: with no connection, a message waits and is sent later, including over a slow channel.
  5. No center: no single point of failure, blocking or seizure.
  6. Node blindness: a node carries only ciphertext and cannot read it (the Zero Knowledge Infrastructure principle).

4.3 How it differs from its predecessors

5. Classification: LL classes

The load class of a node is determined by the fastest-growing of its three components: traffic carried, data stored, service state maintained. N is the number of participants in the network, k is the size of the node's circle: the number of its users and software agents. The notation O(x) ("big O of x"; introduced by P. Bachmann in 1894 [42] and popularized by E. Landau in 1909 [43]) means that a quantity grows no faster than x multiplied by a constant; for class LL-0 the load estimate contains no N, only k.

ClassNode loadDefinition
LL-0O(k)the node carries, stores and remembers only what relates to its circle; no transit for others
LL-1O(k) + bounded foreignplus transit or storage for others, bounded independently of N (hop limit, quotas, neighbors chosen by the node itself)
LL-2O(log N)load grows slowly (distributed hash tables)
LL-3O(N) or not bounded by its circleload grows with the whole network or depends on how many outside users access the node (a popular server, a relay)

A low-load network in the strict sense is a network of class LL-0; LL-1 is almost low-load.

6.2 Observations

  • By the main trait (the LL class) the strictest are Nevod, Briar and Secure Scuttlebutt (LL-0); Reticulum and NNCP belong to LL-1.
  • All centralized and federated networks, as well as anonymity overlay networks, are LL-3: decentralizing ownership does not mean decentralizing load.

7. Nevod as an example of class LL-0, and its limitations

Nevod [34] is a messaging network built on the Zero Knowledge Infrastructure principle: a node serves only its own participants and carries encrypted messages without access to their content; there is no center; nodes run on home hardware or rented servers. The service table of nodes grows with the number of nodes, not users, and is small.

Limitations as of September 2026 (traits not fully met, not proven in practice):

  • trait 3: media is transmitted whole, without previews or download on request;
  • trait 4: undelivered messages are kept for 72 hours; works only over the internet;
  • no forward secrecy: messages are encrypted to the recipient's long-term key;
  • a node sees delivery metadata (who, to whom, when);
  • a working prototype with no real users and no independent audit; closed source; iOS app only.

The traits were derived from Nevod's principles, so the comparison naturally favors it; Nevod's score reflects its architecture, not the maturity of the product.

8. Open questions

  1. Storage for a recipient who is offline for a long time: who stores it and for how long, without breaking class LL-0.
  2. Path finding between a large number of nodes without a central directory.
  3. Is bounded transit (LL-1) acceptable for delivery over slow channels, and is reciprocity needed ("you carry others' traffic, others carry yours")?
  4. Which components count as load: traffic, storage, state, energy use, administrator time.
  5. A formal metric for a node's circle k and its measurement on real networks.

9. Conclusion

The load concepts of communications theory describe the state of a network, not a property of its architecture. The proposed concept of a low-load distributed network singles out an architectural class in which a node's load is bounded by its circle, and complements known approaches (F2F, DTN, distributed hash tables, local-first, community networks) with a combination of six traits. The LL-0…LL-3 classification makes the concept measurable and allows any network to be assigned to a class. Practical validation and a full literature review of closed databases (IEEE, ACM, eLibrary) are the next steps.
